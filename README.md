# Folkboat Racing Sim

A Folkboat racing simulator for learning starting tactics, right of way under the Racing Rules of Sailing, and racing on San Francisco Bay.

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # http://localhost:5173
npm test           # rules + simulation tests
npm run build      # static site in dist/ (relative paths, host anywhere)
npm run data:polar # re-pull Folkboat ORC certificates (behind a proxy: NODE_USE_ENV_PROXY=1)
npm run data:currents # re-pull NOAA tidal-current data for the Cityfront (same proxy note)
npm run data:wind  # rebuild the wind climatology from NOAA's Crissy Field station (same proxy note)
npm run build:artifact  # single-page build for the claude.ai artifact → artifact/folkboat-racing-sim.html
```

**Playing it:** the claude.ai artifact link is the playable version. It's built for desktop (keyboard plus trackpad or mouse); phone layout is on hold. It's rebuilt with `npm run build:artifact` and republished after changes; three.js loads from jsDelivr, pinned to the version in package.json.

The original single-file prototype is kept, unchanged, at `reference/folkboat-sim.html`.

## Controls (desktop)

Every key can be changed: **Settings** (on the start page and the pause screen), or **K** in the game. Your keys are saved in the browser.

- **Tiller.** Click the water in cockpit or chase view, or press **T** in any view, to take the tiller. While you hold it, the cursor's left–right position *is* the tiller handle: the dashed line down the middle of the screen is straight ahead; right of it pushes the handle to starboard, which turns the boat to port (as on a real boat), and left of it turns her to starboard. The further from the centre, the harder over; the solid lines mark hard over. Over buttons and panels the tiller stays put, so you can reach them. Click again, **T** or **Esc** lets go (Esc never pauses while you hold the tiller), and the tiller springs back to centre. You can also hold **A**/**D** to turn to port/starboard. There are no tack or gybe buttons: you put the helm over and hold it until she's round. (Earlier versions captured the mouse; browsers handle that badly, especially inside the claude.ai page.)
- **Views.** **1** cockpit, **2** chase (scroll or **+**/**−** to move closer or farther), **3** overhead, **V** cycles. In cockpit view you look over the helm's shoulder from behind the boat, so you see all of her; scroll (or **+**/**−**) to move your eye fore and aft. It's remembered.
- **Look around.** Arrow keys (or Q/E/R/F) turn your head; **W** looks ahead again. Looking never steers.
- **Overhead.** **N** switches between north up, bow up and wind up (saved). Scroll or **+**/**−** to zoom, drag to move the map, **B** (or the "Back to boat" chip) recentres on your boat.
- **Sails.** **S** luffs (hold), **O** whisker pole, Z/X and , / . work the sheets (taking them off the crew). **I** lashes the tiller where it is.
- **Crew.** The trim panel's **Crew** row (Lee / Mid / Up / Hike, then Aft / Fwd) puts the crew's weight where you want it; **7**/**8** step it to leeward/windward, **9**/**0** forward/aft. Tap the lit one again (or **Auto**) to leave it to them. The line under them says what they're doing.
- **Other.** **H** hail, **M** course view, **L** message log, **P** pause.
- **Chart.** Tap the map at the top right: it opens full screen and the sail pauses. North up; drag to move, scroll to zoom, **Back to boat** recentres. Cruising, tap the water or a named place to see how far it is, its bearing, what point of sail it'll be and the current there now, then **Sail here** to make it your destination. **Esc** closes it.
- **Autopilot (cruising).** **Y** (or the Autopilot chip) engages a tiller pilot holding her heading. Its control head, beside the tiller: **Auto** holds a compass course; **Wind** holds the apparent wind angle; **Nav** sails to your destination, tacking on the laylines (allowing for the current), tacking off the shore, and steering round land in the way. **−10/−1/+1/+10** (or **A**/**D** held) turn the set course; **Tack** turns 100° through the wind in Auto, or puts the same wind angle on the other side in Wind. Taking the tiller (**T**, a click on the water, the tiller bar) puts it on standby. There's none when racing: RRS 52 says a racing boat's rudder is worked only by her crew's own power.
- **Messages.** Notices stay up longer than before, the last few stay in the Messages panel at the right, and **All** (or **L**) opens the whole session's log, including incident explanations.

## Modes

- **Full race:** the start sequence, the course and the fleet.
- **Start practice:** see below.
- **Just sail:** no fleet, no clock. Choose where to start (off Sausalito, Raccoon Strait, Clipper Cove, under the Gate and more) and, if you like, somewhere to sail to: a spot or any of the bay's racing marks and navigation buoys. The compass, the laylines, the map and the readout above the controls then measure to your destination (range, bearing, the speed you're making toward it over the ground, and how long at that rate), and you're told when you get there. The map follows you at a local scale (about 1.5 nm across) rather than showing a race course; the real buoys around the bay are on the water and on the map. The readout also shows the polar target speed for your wind angle and how close you are to it.

### How the pieces fit

- **World** (`sim/world.ts`) owns all race state and advances it with `step(1/60)`. It never touches the DOM or audio; it emits events (`toast`, `horn`, `incident`, `finished`) that `main.ts` turns into UI.
- **Fixed timestep.** `main.ts` accumulates wall-clock time × sim speed and calls `step` in whole 1/60 s steps. Rendering runs at the display rate. With the same seed and inputs a race replays identically (there's a test for this). All sim randomness comes from `World.rng`; `Math.random` is used only for scenery.
- **Rules engine** (`rules/`) is pure: it gets boats (only the fields it needs), a context (race time, wind, marks) and a per-pair record, and returns who keeps clear, under which rule, and any incident. The pair record is the engine's memory (who was inside at the zone, who just gained right of way) and is passed in explicitly.
- **The Seaweed Soup course sheet.** All 41 courses from GGYC's Seaweed Soup 2026–27 Addendum B (courses 1–34 starting west, 41–51 starting east), generated by `scripts/make-seaweed-courses.mjs` from the sheet with the YRA's mark positions. The marks are drawn as what they are: the yellow inflatables (A, X, Fort Mason), the yellow columns (Blackaller, Hank Easom) and the lighted navigation buoys (Knox, Harding Rock's red-green-red, Blossom Rock, the North Channel buoys). Alcatraz is a mark (courses 3, 6, 14, 17, 41, 42): round the island on the side shown; the zone and the rounding are measured from its shore, and the AI works round it in the right direction, giving the rocks a berth and swinging wide (about 300 m) round its downwind side, out of the near-calm in its lee.
- **The race committee's choice** (Course → "The race committee's choice"; `src/courses/committee.ts`). Rule 27.1: the committee signals the course no later than the warning signal; on the Seaweed Soup sheet it's the course number in numeral pennants (Addendum B1.4), flown on the GGYC flagpole and shown in the Signals box. The committee works out how long each of the 41 courses would take the leading Folkboat on the day, the way race officers keep a table of course times by wind strength: every leg at the polar speeds, tacking up the beats and gybing down the runs at the best angles, in the wind and the tide forecast for when the fleet gets there. It picks the course that best fits the target time (Race length: about 30 min, an hour or 90 min), with the first leg to windward and no leg the fleet could barely sail. The setup card and the message log say which course and why. The sheet doesn't say when the courses starting east are used; here the committee starts to windward when it can, and off the wind only when the beat won't work (light air against a strong tide). That rule is our inference. The line (the GGYC flagpole to X) runs north and can't be moved, so in a northerly or a southerly every course starts on a reach. With almost no wind against a big tide a real committee would postpone; here it picks the least bad course. The estimate is calibrated against the sim's own fleet: 15% over the polar arithmetic, plus about six minutes for each Alcatraz rounding.
- **Running aground anywhere.** Beyond the Cityfront (OpenStreetMap's coast from the Gate to Pier 27), the coastline is the real one from the terrain data (50 m cells): Alcatraz, Angel Island, the Marin shore, Treasure Island. The maps draw it all. Reaching or running, the AI steers round land in its way; beating, it tacks off the shore. The AI's and the tiller pilot's look-ahead tests the whole line ahead exactly (`landAlong` in `src/geo/shore.ts`: every pier, breakwater, dock and finger as drawn, the shore's edges and the terrain's cells), so it can't step over a pier or a dock a metre wide.
- **Courses are data.** Every file in `src/courses/data/` shows up in the course picker. Marks have lat/lon (or an offset from the line centre), legs give the rounding side and `round` or `pass`, the start line is either two fixed ends or a centre, bearing and length, and `obstructions` marks the line as an obstruction once racing. See `course.schema.json`.

### Boat model

- **Polar.** Averaged from four current ORC certificates for Nordic Folkboats (NED 737, NED 923, EST F51, EST F39). In 12 kt: 4.7 kt close-hauled at 40.7°, 5.9 kt on a beam reach. Below the beat angle the curve is shaped so that the ORC beat angle really is the best VMG angle; nothing sails below 25°.
- **No spinnaker.** The certificates assume a 24 m² spinnaker; Folkboats race without one. Jib-only downwind speeds are derived from the certificates' own dead-run data: drive ∝ sail area × apparent wind², with main + poled-out jib (26 m²) instead of main + spinnaker (43 m²). Result: 85% of spinnaker speed in light air, 93% near hull speed; 4.3 kt dead downwind in 12 kt. The spinnaker version is still available (`new Polar(data, { spinnaker: true })`).
- **Speed.** Forces, not a fixed lag: drive is set so the boat settles at its polar speed; hull drag comes from the certificate's wetted surface plus wave drag rising toward hull speed; flogging sails and rig windage slow the boat when it luffs or points too high. Calibration (12 kt): 1 kt → 90% speed in ~12 s; luffing halves speed in ~13 s, then the boat stops and loses steerage; shooting head to wind carries ~3.5 lengths; a tack takes ~7 s, slows to ~58% and costs ~0.9 lengths. `tests/boat.test.ts` pins these ranges. All tuning constants are in `HANDLING` in `src/boat/dynamics.ts`.
- **Downwind: wing-on-wing.** The polar has three sail plans: `jib` (sheeted normally, on the main's side: blanketed by the main when deep), `wing` (poled out to windward with the whisker pole: only fills within ~15–20° of dead downwind, aback on a reach) and `main` (jib not drawing while the crew handles the pole). Each plan's speed comes from the sail area actually drawing, using the same dead-run drag model. In 12 kt dead downwind: 3.95 kt normal, 4.26 kt wing-on-wing; poling out pays from ~153° TWA; best downwind VMG ~168°. The crew takes 8 s to set the pole, 5 s to stow it, and gybes it end-for-end (8 s) when you gybe. Use **Pole** or **O**. The HUD suggests the pole when you run deep without it and warns when it's left out on a reach. AI boats pole out when running deep and stow before rounding marks.
- **Build.** Your boat is fiberglass with an aluminium mast and boom; the AI fleet is wooden. Class rules give both the same minimum weight and they race on equal terms, so build changes looks only (`build` in `FLEET`, `src/boat/boat.ts`).
- **Easing sails to slow down** works upwind and reaching (the sails flog). From a broad reach to a run, eased sails still fill against the shrouds, so it barely slows you: head up first.
- **Turning.** Turn rate = speed / turning radius (9 m), up to 22°/s, down to 4°/s when nearly stopped. Turning bleeds speed.
- **Uses of the polar.** AI close-hauled courses, the dial's no-go zone and the laylines use the polar's beat angle (it changes with wind speed) instead of a fixed 42°. The line aid's "time to line" and the AI's start approach assume you sheet in now and accelerate from your current speed, and the AI allows for coasting when it eases sails.

### The strategist and the navigator

**Racing: the strategist** (the course view's Strategist tab, from the setup screen's *Course and strategist* or **M**). A briefing before the start, there to teach you to make the calls yourself, so every point explains why and says where it comes from: NOAA/NWS data, worked out with the sim's physics, general racing practice, or the rules.

- **The day:** a timeline from the warning signal to the estimated finish (the wind, the current at the line, the sea), how the breeze builds or swings (a persistent shift, and which side it favours), where it blows harder or lighter across the course, NOAA's nearest current station's slacks and peaks, the relief along the shore, and wind against tide. How far to trust it: hindsight on an observed day, a forecast, or a typical day from the records.
- **The start:** the favoured end (how many metres and boat lengths, and how to check it on the water), what the current does at the line (carrying you over, or back), how it differs end to end, and which end leads to the favoured side of the first beat.
- **Each leg** sailed on paper three ways (`src/sim/routing.ts`): left first, right first and up the middle, at the polar speeds in the chop, through the day's wind and tide as they change along the way, tacking on the current-bent laylines or off the land. The fastest plan, by how much and why (the current and the wind on each side, the shift during the leg), then tested against the forecast being off (veered or backed 8°, half an hour early or late, a quarter stronger or lighter): a clear call, likely, or open, with a plan B. The plans are drawn on the map. Reaches: how far to steer up-current of the mark.
- **The competition:** each boat's condition and her skipper's habits at the start; your own boat's.
- **Trim and tuning by phase** (start, middle, finish): the settings the boat's own sail model finds fastest for that wind and chop, the upwind target, where the crew sit, and what to change, in what order, as it builds or eases.

**Cruising: the navigator** (**M**, the *Navigator* chip, or *Passage plan* on the setup screen). A passage plan to your destination: the way round the land (`routeAround`, A* on the water), the time leaving now and leaving later (the tide first), the current gates on the way with when each turns (NOAA stations), the strongest and lightest wind (the slot), the ship and ferry routes you cross with what AIS would show and the Inland Navigation Rules for crossing them, and the hazards (rocks by their buoys, sunset and lights, fog signals, cold water).

Both work from `src/sim/outlook.ts`: the day's wind (without the puffs and oscillations nobody can forecast), current and sea at any place and time, from the same data the boats sail in. The tactical rules of thumb are general practice, labelled as such: the session's research couldn't read the local sources (Latitude 38, the sailmakers' Folkboat tuning guides, the Coast Pilot) through the network policy, so local specifics are computed from NOAA data rather than quoted.

### Start practice

Create a race → **Practise the start** (beside *Start the sequence*). You begin one minute before the gun, somewhere below the line. Fifteen seconds after the gun the race pauses for a debrief:

- where you were at the gun (distance from the line, speed as a % of full close-hauled speed, position along the line), when you crossed and your place in the order across the line;
- which end was favored and by how much (in metres upwind);
- a grade (great, good, late, slow, over early) with specific tips (how many seconds to turn earlier, when to sheet in, what the current was doing);
- an overhead replay of the last 45 s, with every boat's position at the gun marked.

**Same start again** replays the identical setup (same seed: same boats, wind and positions). **New start** draws a new one; **Keep racing** carries on with the race. The debrief logic is `src/sim/startDebrief.ts`.

### The race debrief

When you finish, the results show **what cost you time, most first**, in seconds, each with what happened and what to do instead. Pause mid-race for the same thing so far. The log runs as you race (`src/sim/debrief.ts`); every figure is an estimate from the sim's own physics and the usual racing arithmetic:

- **The start:** how late you crossed, where you were and how fast at the gun, and which end was favored (by how many degrees).
- **Wind shifts:** time on a header of more than 4° held for more than 15 s. On a header of θ° your progress upwind is cos(β+θ)/cos(β−θ) of what the other tack would give you (β the close-hauled angle): about a fifth less at 8°. Each tack made while lifted counts too.
- **Laylines:** how far past the layline you went before tacking for each windward mark (from your ground track on the other tack, so the current counts); about 0.6 × the distance over your upwind speed.
- **Tacks and gybes:** your speed lost against target speed for 20 s after each.
- **Dirty air** (and whose) **and the lee of the land:** boat speed goes roughly as wind^0.7 in a Folkboat's range.
- **Downwind:** your speed made good against the best angle, and sailing deep without the pole set.
- **Heel** past her best, **your trim** (when you trim), **penalty turns**, **wide roundings**, and your place at each mark.

Only items worth two seconds or more are listed.

### Rules

Checked against the official 2025–2028 rulebook (World Sailing, with changes and corrections, July 2025). Implemented: 10, 11, 12, 13 (including two boats tacking at once), 15, 16.1, 16.2, 17, 18.1(a), 18.2(a) to (d), 18.3, 18.4, 19, 20 (hail, "You tack" reply, and the hailing boat tacking after the response), 21.1, 21.2, 21.3, 23, 31 (including the committee boat), 43.1(a) and 44.1(a), plus a right-of-way boat forced to swerve (14). Rule numbers follow the 2025 edition: returning to start and taking a penalty are now 21.1 and 21.2 (they were 22.1 and 22.2), and mark-room at the zone is 18.2(a), which ends under 18.2(b) when the entitled boat passes head to wind or leaves the zone.

- **Windward and leeward** are sides, as in the Definitions: on starboard tack the boat on the other's starboard side is windward, whoever is farther upwind. (Close-hauled, a boat half a length ahead and a few metres to leeward is still the leeward boat.)
- **18.1(a):** no mark-room between boats on opposite tacks on a beat to windward; port/starboard decides.
- **18.3 (tacking in the zone):** a boat that passes head to wind from port to starboard inside the zone of a mark left to port, near a starboard boat that is fetching the mark, gets no mark-room from her (18.2 doesn't apply between them). If that boat has been on starboard since entering the zone, the tacker must not make her sail above close-hauled to avoid contact, and gives her mark-room if she gets inside. "Fetching" is approximated as being able to reach a point just to windward of the mark on starboard without sailing above close-hauled.
- **18.2(c) and (d):** once 18.2(a) has ended (the entitled boat passed head to wind or left the zone), an outside boat still gives an inside overlapped boat mark-room while one of them is in the zone. Not if the inside boat got her overlap from clear astern or by tacking to windward when the outside boat couldn't give room from that moment (no room between her and the mark).
- **18.4 (gybing in the zone):** an inside overlapped boat with right of way that must gybe at the mark to sail the next leg may not sail on past it, farther from the mark than she needs, taking the outside boat with her.
- **19 (room to pass an obstruction):** the shore (with the Fort Mason piers) and, after the start, the committee boat. When boats are overlapped there, the outside boat gives the inside boat room between her and the obstruction (19.2(b)); squeezing her toward it is the outside boat's foul, unless there was no room from the moment the overlap began. The inside boat still keeps clear in other ways (bearing down onto the outside boat is still her foul). Along the shore, a continuing obstruction, a boat that comes from clear astern into a gap that isn't there gets no room and keeps clear (19.2(c)). Before the start the committee boat is a starting mark, not an obstruction, so there's no room for barging there.
- **21.3:** a boat moving astern or sideways to windward by backing a sail keeps clear. The rule is in the engine, but the boats can't sail backwards yet, so it doesn't come up.
- **23:** a boat that has finished (not racing) mustn't interfere with boats still racing (23.1); and you mustn't go out of your way (turning toward her, or above your proper course after the start) to hinder a boat on another leg, doing turns or returning to start (23.2). Sailing your proper course past her is fine.
- **31 (touching a mark):** touching a starting mark before you start (the committee boat too, when the line has one), a mark of the leg you're sailing (the one you're heading for or the one you just left), or a finishing mark is a one-turn penalty (one circle, with a tack and a gybe), whatever the Part 2 penalty setting. The AI gives marks room and takes its turns away from them. Boats bounce off the committee boat whenever they hit it.
- **43.1(a) (exoneration):** if another boat's foul compelled you to break a rule (she forced you to swerve, and you swerved into a third boat), you're exonerated: no penalty, and the replay says so. Approximated as: you fouled within 3 s of being fouled by a different boat.
- **44.1(a):** a Part 2 foul and a mark touch in the same incident (within 8 s) cost only the Part 2 penalty.

- **16.1:** a right-of-way boat that turned more than 12° toward the other boat in the 3 s before an incident didn't give her room to keep clear, and is the one penalized. Slow, steady course changes are fine; turning away is fine.
- **16.2:** on a beat, a starboard boat that bears away onto a port boat ducking behind her breaks 16.2 (16.1 covers other course changes).
- **17:** if a boat clear astern gets an overlap to leeward within two lengths, she may not sail above her proper course while that overlap lasts (after the start signal; there's no proper course before it). "Proper course" is approximated as straight at the next mark if she can lay it, close-hauled if not, and no deeper than the best downwind angle. The situation banner says when Rule 17 restricts you or the other boat.
- **20 and hails:** close-hauled (or above) and soon to need a substantial course change to clear an obstruction, with a boat on the same tack to windward stopping you tacking, you may hail "Room to tack!" (**Hail** / **H**); the hailed boat must start to tack within 5 s. It works at every obstruction: the Cityfront shore, the Wharf's piers and breakwaters, the marinas' docks, Alcatraz, Angel Island, the Marin shore and Treasure Island (`src/sim/obstruction.ts`). "Soon" is when you'd have to start the whole business (WS Case 54): land ahead, across your whole width, within two response times at your speed plus two and a half lengths to tack clear (about 45 m at 5 kt). "Substantial" is taken as more than 10° (the rules give no figure; Dave Perry's US Sailing guide to the 2025–2028 rules: "a course change of less than 10 degrees is not very 'substantial'"): if bearing away 10° clears it (a pier head), you sail round it instead. Press Hail when you may not and it says which part of 20.1 stops you. Not at an island that is the mark when the boat you'd hail is fetching it (20.1's last sentence); and while a hail is under way, mark-room (18.2) doesn't apply between the two boats (20.2(e)). AI boats pinned against any of them hail you, a banner counts down, and ignoring it is a foul. An AI boat pinned before it may hail holds on until it may, and bears away round an island mark you're fetching rather than tack into you. The hailed boat may instead reply "You tack" at once and then give the hailing boat room to tack and pass astern (20.2(c)): when an AI boat hails you, **H** replies "You tack"; when you hail an AI boat that is clear ahead with room for you to tack under her stern, she replies "You tack" and you should tack. Rule 15's grace doesn't apply to a reply (it's the other boat's own action). Once the hailed boat responds (by tacking or replying), the hailing boat must tack as soon as possible (20.2(d)); the game gives 5 s for each response. On starboard with a port boat converging, Hail calls "Starboard!" and she ducks earlier. Hail when you're not entitled to and it explains why not.
- **Penalties.** A Part 2 foul (Rules 10–22) costs a two-turns penalty (RRS 44.1): two full turns, each with a tack and a gybe, in the same direction. Turns only count in one direction: wiggling back and forth doesn't finish it. Sailing instructions sometimes reduce this to one turn, so the host club's sailing instructions (Create a race → Host club → *Change*) have a **Penalty** setting (two turns, the rulebook default, or one). Touching a mark (Rule 31) is always one turn. Being on the course side at the start under SI B1.1 is still two turns.
- **Starting signals (Rules 26, 29.1, 30):** the race committee flies the real flags, on the committee boat's signal mast or on a yardarm on the GGYC flagpole, with the sounds: class flag up at the warning signal, the preparatory flag at the next minute, preparatory flag down with a long sound at one minute (in a 3-minute sequence), class flag down at the gun. If anyone is over, the X flag goes up with one sound (individual recall) until they've all returned, four minutes at most. Choose the preparatory flag in the sailing instructions (Create a race → Host club → *Change*): **P** (the usual start), **I** (over in the last minute: go back round an end of the line, 30.1), **Z** (in the triangle formed by the line ends and the first mark in the last minute: 20% scoring penalty, 30.2), **U** (in the triangle: disqualified, 30.3) or the **black flag** (disqualified, even after a general recall, 30.4). A signals panel under the wind dial shows the flags flying and the next signal; the message log explains each one. The AI starts a little more cautiously under the stricter flags. At a start to leeward (the courses starting east in a westerly) the AI holds upwind of the line and comes in on a run, on starboard gybe when it can; since easing the sails barely slows a running boat, it burns time on a reach along the line, easing there, and bears away for the line when it's time, allowing for the current. Before the start, AI boats tack and gybe only where it won't put them across another boat's bows, and a right-of-way boat turns only as far as still leaves the other boat room (Rule 16.1). Results show ZFP, UFD and BFD.
- **Marks** heel over downstream on their moorings in the current and trail a wake, so you can read the current at the mark.
- Incidents before the start signal now count. (In the prototype, a timer started at −1 s, so collisions before the gun were never judged.)

### The start page

Three ways in: **Race**, **Cruise** and **Fleet** (your boats: their records; build, set up, race or cruise one), plus **Settings** (training aids, how to sail her, the keyboard) and the Yacht Club (coming: crew and skippers). **Exit** (in the controls, and in the pause menu) leaves any mode for the start page.

**Race** → **Create a race** (on any day: a past day sails in its observed weather) or **Run a past race**: one of your own races from the logbook, sailed again. Each race you save keeps its setup with it (the seed, so the same boats, wind and positions; the course; the fleet, crew, start time and conditions settings), so it's the same race; an entry from before that keeps its course, day and start time. The day's weather is the sim's record of that day now, so a race sailed on the forecast is sailed again in what was observed. Create a race is one screen: your choices on the left, in order (1 host club, with its sailing instructions behind *Change*: sequence, preparatory flag, penalty, and the line bias in the practice area; 2 course: the committee's choice and a race length, or one you pick; 3 competitors; 4 your boat and crew, and whether she starts by the line or in her slip; 5 the date and start time), and on the right the conditions for that day and time with the tide graph, and the buttons: *Course and strategist*, *Practise the start*, *Start the sequence*. **Cruise** is the same with from, to, your boat and crew, and when you leave; *Passage plan* and *Cast off*. **Back to setup** (pause, results, the start-practice debrief) returns to it with your choices as they were.

Race and Cruise sail the real day (below). **Change the conditions…** (on the conditions card) changes any of it: the kind of wind, wind speed, wind direction, the current, the chop (× the real chop, down to glassy), the swell outside the Gate, rain (light or heavy: overcast light, rain streaks, less visibility), fog (the real summer fog bank, none, patchy or thick) and ship traffic. Each starts as Real; Real puts it back, and the card says *adjusted* until everything is real again.

### Your saves

Your boats, settings, keys and logbook are saved as you go. On the published sim they're also kept with your claude.ai account, in a private part of the artifact's database that only you can read (`src/store/cloud.ts`), so a new version of the sim, another browser or another device brings them back. The newer copy of each save wins; nothing is written to your account until it has been read, so a browser that has lost its storage can't overwrite your saves with a fresh start. Outside claude.ai (the dev server, a saved copy) they stay in the browser. The start page says which.

When a race or a cruise ends (you leave it, start another, or go to setup), you're asked whether to **save** it to her logbook (her race record and crew history) or **discard** it.

### The tiller pilot

`src/boat/autopilot.ts`: an electric ram on the tiller, modelled on the common Raymarine ST2000+ (rated to 4,500 kg displacement; hard over to hard over in about 4.5 s, from secondary sources: the handbook itself wasn't reachable), so it can't correct faster than that, and in a seaway it works all the time. It steers a turn rate proportional to the heading error, and learns the standing helm (weather helm) she needs and carries it. Not when racing: RRS 52 says movable hull appendages are worked only by the crew's power, and the Equipment Rules (E.1.2(j)) make the rudder one; World Sailing's Offshore Special Regulations Appendix M shows the notice-of-race change that allows autopilots offshore. In Wind mode it reads the masthead unit damped over 2.5 s, as a pilot's response setting does. Nav is the pilot plus a navigator calling the tacks. It keeps no lookout: other boats, ships and the shore ahead on a reach are still yours. Tested: holding course on a beam reach in a real seaway (within 3°, tiller within 17% of its travel), no standing error close-hauled in 20 kt, and beating to Crissy Field from the Cityfront in 11 minutes with two tacks.

### The marinas

Five harbors, each from OpenStreetMap: its basin (water you can sail in), its breakwaters, its docks (main walkways and slip fingers) and solid piers, and its entrance beacons. Docks and breakwaters are solid (you bump the dock). Slips aren't mapped, so they're worked out from the fingers: those a Folkboat fits in (her length and beam clear of every dock). Floating wooden docks, timber pilings and generic boats in about three-quarters of the slips.

- **West Harbor**, San Francisco Marina, by the St. Francis and Golden Gate yacht clubs (`scripts/make-west-harbor.mjs`): the entrance at the east end by its red beacon (Q R); 284 slips. Dock letters and slip numbers are this sim's: the harbor's own aren't published anywhere we could reach.
- **Gashouse Cove**, the Marina's East Harbor, by Fort Mason (`scripts/make-marinas.mjs`, like the two below): the seawall along its north side, hooking out to the entrance light (East Yacht Harbor Light 2, Iso R 6s), on the line NOAA's chart has it (ENC US5CA13M; OSM tags the wall as a pier): vertical concrete about 1.8 m above the water, with a walkway and a white pipe railing on top, A dock alongside its inside face (its width, 3 m and 2 m for the hook, isn't charted); the way in between the hook's end and Fort Mason's Pier 1. Inside it, the chop and swell are what gets past the walls (the sea model traces the waves out of a basin every 2 m); 313 slips on docks A–G. Dock letters (north to south) and slip numbers are this sim's.
- **South Beach Harbor**, by Oracle Park: the breakwater along the east side with a way in at each end between lateral beacons (red triangles, green squares); 676 slips (the Port of San Francisco says about 700; the rest don't come out of the fingers as mapped). Docks A–G (north to south) are the harbor's own names; slip numbers are this sim's, along each dock from the shore.
- **The Corinthian Yacht Club**, Tiburon: the basin behind the club's L-shaped breakwater, open to the north-east between its end and the ferry floats; 58 slips. A dock is the southernmost line, along the breakwater, with the guest dock at its end (the club's visitor notes); B and C, the fingers (not mapped) and the slip numbers are inferred.
- **Travis Marina, Horseshoe Bay** (Fort Baker, by the Presidio Yacht Club): the bay behind its rubble breakwater, with a paved path along the top, open to the west toward the Golden Gate Bridge; floating docks on white piles: an L dock off the east shore and a row inside the breakwater; 79 slips on docks A–C (the sim's letters, north to south). The basin is the water inside OSM's marina outline (which takes in Marina Road and the north shore's buildings), its breakwater as NOAA charts it (ENC US5CA13M), with the paved path along its top that OSM maps (`scripts/make-horseshoe-basin.py` works out the basin).

In the **Fleet**, give any of your boats a home marina and a slip in it. In **Cruise**, start from her slip (bow in, made fast: push off to leave) and/or sail back to it: you're home when she's nosed in and stopped. In **Race**, start from her slip with time to sail out to the line (from South Beach or Tiburon, 5–7 km away, up to two hours before the warning signal: speed up the clock).

### The real day: observed, forecast, or from the records

Change the conditions → Kind of wind → **The real day** (the default) races in the weather the race day really had or will have (`src/environment/weather.ts`, data from `scripts/fetch-real-days.mjs`):

- **A past day** (every day since January 2021): the wind observed at Fort Point every 6 minutes from 10:00 to 18:00 (NOAA CO-OPS 9414290: the 2-minute mean speed and direction, and the gust), so you sail that day's actual shifts, lulls and gustiness; and the swell observed at the San Francisco Bar buoy (NDBC 46237). On 6 December 2025, a Seaweed Soup day, that's a 10–14 kt northerly.
- **The coming week:** the National Weather Service gridded forecast, hour by hour: the wind on the 2.5 km grid cell over the Cityfront race area, and the swell at the cell by the Bar buoy. The shifts and puffs within each hour are the sim's own.
- **Any other day:** a day drawn from the five years of records for its month (below).
- **The wind across the course.** The wind isn't the same everywhere on the Bay, and the side you pick is half of Bay racing, so the sim works out the wind across the course, not one number (`dayWeather().field` in `src/environment/weather.ts`):
  - *a forecast day:* the National Weather Service's own forecast for every 2.5 km grid cell over the race area (56 cells, hour by hour), relative to the cell over the Cityfront; the sim adds only what the grid is too coarse to see (the slowdown along the beach, Alcatraz's wind shadow);
  - *a past day:* the lie of the land (below: the slot, the shore, the lee of the islands and headlands) driven by Fort Point, then nudged toward what NOAA's other stations around the Bay saw that day (Pier 1 on the Embarcadero, Oakland Middle Harbor, Point Potrero, Richmond: hourly). Each station is compared with how it usually reads against Fort Point on that kind of day at that hour (five years of records), since a sheltered station reads low every day: only how today differs is news about the wind on the water. The nudges fade with distance (an objective analysis, the way gridded observed-wind analyses are made). On 12 July 2025 at 14:00 that gives 11–13 kt at the Gate and along the Cityfront and 15–17 kt east of Alcatraz toward Blossom Rock, veering from 231° to 245°;
  - with training aids on, the map shades where it's blowing harder (darker) and lighter, with a tick for which way it blows.
- **Keeping it current:** a scheduled task fetches the observations since the newest day in the repository's data and the latest forecast every morning, and republishes the sim (the repository's copy of the data is brought up to date whenever it's next committed). The setup note says where the day's weather comes from (and when the forecast was issued).
- The tide and current are NOAA's own predictions for any date (below), from every NOAA current station in the race area; at each station the sim's current is that station's prediction to within 0.05 kt (tested through a whole tidal day). NOAA's live current meters are all outside the race area (Oakland, Southampton Shoal), so a past day's current is still the prediction.

### The day's wind: the westerly, the winter winds, puffs and lulls

Change the conditions → **Kind of wind** (the real day, or a westerly, northerly, southerly, or light and variable day from the records, to practise in) and **Wind** (the day's wind, or a set 8, 12 or 18 kt in the day's direction; for a day from the records, a light, typical or windy day of its kind). It uses the same race day and start time as the tide (the setup's date and start time), and the graph shows the wind (green line) as well as the current.

- **From the records.** Five years (2021–2025, 43,745 hours) of NOAA's hourly wind at its San Francisco station (9414290, on the shore at Fort Point and Crissy Field, the west end of the race area), `scripts/fetch-wind-climate.mjs` → `src/environment/data/sf-wind-climate.json`. Each day is classed by its wind over the racing hours (11:00–17:00): the **westerly** sea breeze (from 200–330°), a **northerly** (330–100°: behind a cold front, or the dry offshore wind), a **southerly** (100–200°: ahead of a storm), or **light and variable** (under 4 kt on average, or no settled direction). For each month the records give how often each kind of day comes, and for each kind, hour by hour, how hard it blows (25th, 50th and 75th percentile: a light, typical or windy day), from where, how steady and how gusty.
- **Winter isn't the westerly.** From April to September it's the westerly nearly every day. In November it's 43% westerly, 29% northerly, 9% southerly and 19% light; in December 19% westerly, 48% northerly (about 8–9 kt at the station from about 10°), 11% southerly and 22% light; January is a mix. With Weather on "from the records", each race date draws its kind of day from its month (the same date always gives the same weather); the setup note says the odds and what you got.
- **Through the race.** With the wind from the records, its strength and direction follow the clock (in July the westerly builds from about 7 kt at 11:00 to about 12 kt at 16:00 at the station). A light and variable day swings much further, and wanders over ten minutes or so. Puffs follow the kind of day's gustiness.
- **The course in the day's wind.** A course laid from its line (the training course) is set to the wind. The Seaweed Soup marks are fixed, so the legs are whatever the wind makes them: in a northerly the first leg to A is a reach. Which legs are beats (for Rule 18, the laylines, the debrief and the course view) follows the day's wind.
- **The slot.** Up to 20% more wind along a band from the Golden Gate toward Alcatraz and Angel Island, where the westerly funnels in (only in a westerly: a northerly or southerly doesn't come through the Gate); less close to the Cityfront.
- **Puffs and lulls.** Patches of stronger and lighter wind, longer downwind than across, drifting downwind at a bit less than the wind speed and fading in and out over one to three minutes. A puff is up to about a third stronger and veered a few degrees (wind brought down from aloft is veered in the northern hemisphere); a lull is lighter. How strong they are follows the gust factor in the records. Every boat feels the wind where she is.
- **Wind shadows** (`src/environment/shadows.ts`): what the wind records don't know about.
  - *Dirty air.* Every boat's sails leave a wake along the apparent wind she sails in (the masthead fly shows you which way): upwind about 15–19% less wind a mast height behind her, 8–12% at two, 4–6% at four, and far more behind a running boat (her sails are mostly drag). The air is turned too: up to about 7° of header right behind her for a boat on her tack, about 3° in the "safe leeward" spot, a small lift just ahead of her. Luffing sails make almost none. From a Gaussian momentum-wake model and a lifting-line fit, checked against CFD and wind-tunnel studies of yachts sailing together (±50%). The readout under the wind says "dirty air −12%"; with training aids on, the map shows each boat's shadow as a faint cone. AI boats bear away out of dirty air on the way to the line, and tack out of it on a beat.
  - *Ships and the committee boat* leave bluff-body wakes: a near-calm cavity in a container ship's lee, about 70–90% of the wind 10–15 ship heights downwind; a fast ferry's wake is a narrow strip along her track; the committee boat matters only a few lengths downwind of her.
  - *The lee of the land:* the islands and headlands the sea breeze blows over (from the real terrain): Angel Island's lee reaches 3–5 km, Alcatraz's about half a kilometre east of it, the Marin hills' over Sausalito. The slot isn't sheltered by the hills either side of the Gate (that air came through the gap), and the Cityfront is left to the station's own records. The readout says "in the lee −20%", and the water there is drawn smoother and paler.
- **Seeing them.** On the water, puffs are darker and rougher with denser ripples (rough water reflects less of the bright sky), lulls paler and smoother, with whitecaps from about 12 kt; the ripples lie across the wind. They show as dark and pale streaks on the course map and course view, and the wind readout says "puff" or "lull".
- **Presets.** 8, 12 and 18 kt are still there (steady mean wind, with the same slot, shore and puffs).
- Not modelled yet: the wind bending along the shore, the fog and the evening fade beyond what the hourly records show, and the AI deliberately sailing to the pressure.

### Real tides and currents

Current: **Real tide** (the default), for the date and start time you pick in the setup. A graph shows the current at the start line through the day, with the gun and the length of a race marked, and a line like "Ebb 0.8 kt, building · turns to flood ~22:45".

- **Where it comes from.** NOAA's tidal-current predictions for every NOAA current station in the race area, 34 in all, from the Golden Gate to Treasure Island and Raccoon Strait: 14 harmonic stations from NOAA's 2012–13 San Francisco Bay current survey (SFB1202–SFB1212, SFB1217 and their references) and 20 subordinate stations (around Alcatraz, Angel Island and Point Blunt, Yellow Bluff, Fort Point and Crissy Field, Treasure Island, Raccoon Strait), each at its published near-surface bin. `scripts/fetch-currents.mjs` downloads each station's tidal constants once (8 KB); the game then predicts the current for any date and time with no network, by summing the tidal constituents with their yearly corrections (`src/environment/harmonics.ts`). The subordinate stations are derived from their reference station's slacks and peaks with NOAA's time and speed offsets. The tests check the predictions against NOAA's own: within about 0.03–0.08 kt typically (worst case under 0.2 kt).
- **Across the water.** The station currents are blended by distance, then eased near the shore (25% at the beach, full strength 350 m out), since no station sits right on the Marina Green or Fort Mason shoreline.
- **Through the race.** The current changes as the race goes on: the HUD clock shows the local time, the course map draws the current across the water (arrows longer where it's stronger), and the map corner shows the current where you are.
- **Presets.** Slack, flood and ebb presets (uniform, with the same shore relief) are still there for practice.
- Not modelled yet: eddies behind points, and the difference between surface and deeper current.

### Sea state

- **Chop** from the wind over the water and the fetch (open water upwind, measured on the real coastline, averaged across the directions around the wind as the Shore Protection Manual does), by the standard fetch-limited growth laws (JONSWAP). The Central Bay's fetch is short, so the westerly makes a short, steep chop: about 1 ft at the start line in a 20 kt westerly at slack, 1.5 ft on a 2 kt ebb, more out in the channel.
- **Wind against tide falls out of the physics.** Waves are made by the wind over the *water*: an ebb under a westerly adds to that wind and slows the waves over the ground, so they stay under the wind longer and grow bigger; a flood does the opposite. Where waves run into a stronger opposing current they shorten and steepen (wave action is conserved), and if it's too strong for them they break.
- **Swell** from the San Francisco Bar buoy (NDBC 46237, 2019–2025): a small, typical or big day for the month (about 2 m at 13 s in winter, 1.1 m in late summer, outside the Gate). It comes in through the Gate, fans out and dies away inside, and the islands and points shadow it. How fast it dies is an estimate: there's no wave buoy inside the Central Bay to check it against.
- **What it does to the boat:** added resistance in waves (the standard ρ·g·H²·B²/L form), worst head-on and with waves about a boat-length long, which is exactly the Bay's chop. A pinching boat loses a bigger share of her speed to it, so **footing pays in a seaway**: the best upwind angle widens by a couple of degrees in 2 ft of chop and more in light air or bigger seas. The AI, the laylines, the tack targets and the speed target all sail to it. Calibrated so 2 ft of boat-length chop costs about a tenth of a Folkboat's upwind speed: a judgement, not a measurement. The sea also knocks her bow about, most with it on the quarter.
- **On the water:** the same waves you sail in, drawn as a sum of Gerstner waves (chop and swell, heights from the wave spectrum where you are, bigger or smaller elsewhere from a sea map), with whitecaps on the crests from about 15 kt (more where the chop is steep), and the sky's reflection off each wave face. The boats heave, pitch and roll on them. The cockpit view rides with the hull but keeps the horizon level.
- **Tidelines:** where the current really converges (computed from the current field: where it slows or turns, above about 0.5 m/s per kilometre), a thin, broken line of foam, weed and debris, with a slightly smoother slick inside it. You see them along the edge of the Cityfront's current relief and where the water inshore turns before the channel does. Only as sharp as the current model (stations a few hundred metres to a mile apart, blended), so they're broad features, not every small rip.
- **Sails and finish:** Dacron sails with crosscut panel seams, the class letter near the head and the sail number on the main (starboard side above port, as Appendix G of the rules asks), reading the right way round from both sides. Gelcoat, varnish and alloy spars reflect the sky.
- **Your boats and your fleet.** In Create a race, pick the boat you'll sail and tick the boats you'll race against: the club boats, and any of your own saved boats (the AI sails them as you set them up: a rival with a dirty bottom and tired sails really is slower). Under *More club Folkboats*, **+ Add one** fills out the fleet with generic club Folkboats, each always the same boat (her name, sail number and colours) and kept a little differently (a bottom a few weeks from its scrub, sails new to a few seasons old, some gear or an outboard aboard), which the physics feels; tap one to drop her. Up to seven opponents. *Your boats…* opens the boat panel: name, save, copy and delete as many boats as you like, set up any of them (yours or a rival), *Sail this boat* or *Race against her*; every change is saved as you make it. The panel sits beside the boat, which turns slowly in a showroom view as you change her.
  - *Looks* (no effect on speed): built in fibreglass or wood (the class weighs both to the same 1930 kg minimum); her name; topsides (24 colours, and varnished planking on a wooden boat); an optional boot stripe; bottom paint; the deck (any of the colours, or silvered or polished laid teak on a wooden boat); her sail number with World Sailing national letters; white or black sails.
  - *Flags*, flown by yachting etiquette (USPS, CCA, RYA) when you're just sailing (racing boats strike them for a race): the ensign on a staff at the stern, on the starboard quarter clear of the backstay and tiller (US flag, US yacht ensign, or 13 others drawn to their official constructions), the club burgee (18 Bay Area clubs, from Flags of the World) or an officer's flag in its place at the starboard spreader. Under a foreign ensign she flies the US flag as a courtesy flag at the starboard spreader and moves the burgee to port. Owner-absent, guest and meal flags are for a boat at anchor, so they're not offered.
  - *Condition* (changes her speed, with what each costs measured live by sailing her in the sim):
    - **Bottom: weeks since cleaned, and the paint's finish.** Fouling roughness from Schultz (2007): slime builds over the first weeks, barnacles come once copper antifouling gives out (about six weeks), faster in late-summer water. The roughness raises skin friction through the standard roughness functions (Colebrook for slime, which measured biofilm drag follows; Cebeci–Bradshaw for hard growth). It hurts most in light air, where friction is most of her drag: two weeks of slime costs about 5–8% in light air, 3–5% in a breeze. A burnished racing finish is worth a fraction of a percent.
    - **Sails: new, midlife or end of life, main and jib separately.** Aged Dacron sits deeper with the draft further aft, stretches more as the apparent wind loads it, twists open at the head in the gusts and hooks at the leech (more profile drag): more heel and weather helm, less drive. The crew can't flatten an old main back (the outhaul stops at its band), so old sails cost several percent upwind in 20 kt, up to about 15% with both sails at the end of their life, much less in light air (the sizes of the stretch are estimates).
    - **Mast and boom: aluminium or wood, and a wooden mast's stiffness.** The class designed the aluminium mast to bend like an average wooden one (aluminium masts are all alike; wooden ones measure about 105–145 mm in the class bend test, aluminium about 125). A soft mast starts flatter, flattens further under backstay, and bends under the sail's load, so the main flattens and its upper leech opens by itself in a gust: less heel in the gusts, a little less power in moderate air. A stiff mast keeps its power and heels more. A wooden rig is also about 9 kg heavier aloft. In the sim, a stiff mast comes out slightly ahead in a moderate gusty breeze (its extra power in the gusts is worth more than the heel it costs) and the differences are a fraction of a percent in steady wind (the wooden rig's 9 kg aloft costs about 1% in 20 kt, where her stability holds her back): the popular view that a soft mast pays in a breeze isn't borne out here, and both models are estimates. The boom makes no measured difference.
    - **Outboard: ashore, stowed below, or on the transom bracket** (about 13 kg; on the bracket, weight at the very stern, and against the class rules while racing). **Weight over the class minimum:** gear and stores, or an old wooden hull's soaked planking: slower in light air, a little stiffer in a breeze.
  - The AI fleet sails clean, new-sailed boats: the reference the costs are measured against.
- **Crew:** people, not blocks: jointed figures (hips, knees, elbows) sized to each person's height and build, in spray tops and life jackets. They go where the crew physics puts them: to leeward and forward in light air, sitting up to windward in a breeze, hiking (hips over the deck edge, legs inside: the class rules allow no straps, and upwind the legs stay inboard) only when she needs it, the pole hand kneeling at the mast downwind, ducking across under the boom after a tack, at the winch tailing the jib sheet, kneeling at the cabin-top lines, out on the side deck by the jib car. Each is drawn where the physics has their weight. The helm sits at the tiller. In the cockpit view your own helm is hidden: that's you.
- **Anchored and moored boats** in Richardson Bay off Sausalito, Belvedere Cove and Clipper Cove, each lying to the balance of the current on her keel and the wind on her topsides. Water is about 840 times denser than air, so they point into the current unless it's slack: read the current from them, as on the bay. (Scenery: you sail through them. The marinas themselves aren't open water in the sim's coastline yet.)
- **Wakes, bow waves and spray:** every boat leaves a foamy wash behind her stern that widens and fades, and the two arms of the Kelvin wave pattern spreading at 19.5° either side of her track; a bow wave curls off the stem, bigger as she speeds up; in a chop she throws spray when her bow drops into a wave.
- **The water texture flows with the current** at each point (flow mapping), so you can see the water moving faster out in the channel than inshore.
- Not yet: eddies behind points and boils over shoals. The current model doesn't have them (that needs NOAA's bay model output), so they aren't drawn.
- **Settings:** real waves or flat water, and the swell day. The HUD shows the chop and swell where you are.
- Data: `scripts/fetch-swell.mjs` (`npm run data:swell`) for the buoy climatology; `scripts/make-land-mask.mjs` makes the coastline mask from the terrain.

### Ship traffic

- **Wildlife**, in the numbers you'd see from a boat (`src/render/wildlife.ts`; visual only):
  - **Harbor porpoises** in pairs and small groups where they feed in the tidal flow (by the Gate, along the Marin side, Raccoon Strait): two to four quick rolls showing a dark back and small triangular fin (paler grey on the flanks), then a dive of half a minute to two; they keep clear of boats.
  - **Harbor seals:** a mottled grey head floating upright, nose up, turning to watch the nearest boat; a minute or so up, a few minutes down, and down at once if you come close.
  - **California sea lions** on the Pier 39 floats: most from autumn to spring, fewest in June and July, when they've gone south to breed.
  - **Birds,** with jointed wings beating at their real rates and banking into their turns: western gulls (white, slate-grey wings with black tips) wheeling and gliding overhead with spells of flapping, and sitting on the water until you sail up; lines of brown pelicans (grey-brown, white heads, long bills) a metre above the waves in ground effect, a few slow beats and a long glide, now and then climbing and plunging bill-first for a fish, from about June to November; double-crested cormorants, black with their necks out, flying fast and low with quick steady beats.
- **Ships, ferries and tour boats** on approximate real tracks (`src/sim/traffic.ts`): a container ship and a tanker in the deep water from the Gate across the Central Bay toward Oakland; the Sausalito ferry from the Ferry Building; the Alcatraz ferry from Pier 33; two bay-cruise boats from Pier 41, out along the Cityfront, under the bridge and back; and a Coast Guard boat out of Horseshoe Bay. Built to their real lengths and speeds; they keep their schedule through the day, and they're on the course map with a line showing where they'll be in a minute.
- **Their wakes** are the Kelvin pattern: two arms at 19.47° either side of the track, the same angle for any vessel at any speed. The waves are bigger for bigger, faster vessels, die away slowly as the arms spread, and arrive well after the vessel has passed. When an arm reaches you it's a short burst of steep waves: you slow and the bow gets knocked about, through the same wave physics as the chop.
- **The collision regulations.** The racing rules don't apply between you and a ship; the collision regulations do, and a sailing boat must not impede a vessel that can only navigate in the channel (Rule 9). As one closes on you, a message says so; if you're standing into her way she sounds five short blasts, the danger signal. Hitting one stops you dead. The AI boats keep clear by passing astern. Switch traffic off under Change the conditions.

### Sun, sky, the land and the landmarks

- **The sun where it really is** for the race day and the clock (NOAA's solar position algorithm, for the Cityfront; `src/environment/sun.ts`, tested against noon heights and sunset times). It sets the light's direction, colour (warm and low late in the day) and strength, and the shadows: sails shade the deck and the water.
- **The sky** is a physical sky model (Preetham, from three.js) with scattered cloud, exposed for this renderer; after sunset a twilight glow toward the sun fades over the next half hour or so, and the light dims and cools without going black (you can still sail). The haze toward the horizon matches the sky: blue-grey by day, warm near sunset, deep blue at dusk.
- **On the water:** the sun's glitter path (the ripples break the reflection into sparkles), and the sky reflected at a glancing angle, so the water toward the horizon goes pale. Rough water reflects less of the sky (its ripples tilt toward you), so from the deck a puff is a dark band on the water coming down toward you, with ragged edges that travel with it, and a lull a pale, glassy one; in light air the wind comes in cat's paws.

### Sails and trim

- **Trim physics** (`src/boat/trim.ts`). Each sail has a sheet angle. Its angle of attack is the apparent wind angle minus that; each sail drives best at one angle of attack (main 22°, jib 18°). Ease past it and the sail luffs (drive falls fast: gone 12° past); trim past it and it stalls (drive falls slowly, to about half). From a broad reach to a run the sails just want easing right out. Pointing too high luffs the jib even with the sheet hard in.
- **The sails' forces** (`src/boat/balance.ts`) come from ORC's published aerodynamic model (VPP Documentation 2025, chapter 5): the mainsail and jib lift and drag tables, the rig's effective height and induced drag, flattening and twist, the jib blanketed off the wind, on the Folkboat's measured rig (ORC certificates and the class rig drawing: P 8.75, E 3.38, J 2.00, jib luff 5.95, LP 2.36 m). The wind the sails see is the wind at their height (a log profile from the 10 m reading) less her motion, with the part across the boat shortened as she heels (ORC's wind triangle), plus the rig swinging as she rolls.
- **Every line on the boat** (`src/boat/controls.ts`), laid out as on Josephine:
  - *Cockpit thwart* (a wooden plank across the cockpit, in front of the helmsman): mainsheet and traveller. Together they set the boom angle (the car, under the end of the boom, swings it about ±9.5°); the sheet's tension closes the leech: hard while it runs nearly straight down from the boom end to the car, less as the boom swings outboard of the car (cars to windward and the sheet eased in light air opens the leech; cars in the middle and the sheet tight in medium air closes it).
  - *Thwart ends:* backstay, a dual-ended control cleated at each end of the thwart where it meets the hull, so you can reach it from either side. The bridle on the transom flattens the main by bending the mast, and the jib by tightening the forestay.
  - *Piano* (aft end of the cabin): vang (holds the leech closed once the sheet is eased; matters most off the wind), outhaul (depth in the lower main: 3 cm off the black band to on it, about 1.5% of camber), Cunningham and downhaul (luff tension: pull the draft forward), jib Cunningham.
  - *Winches:* the jib sheet, hauled in on the leeward winch: slower as the breeze builds (not self-tailing), quick to ease.
  - *Cabin top:* a jib car each side on an H-shaped track, pinned fore–aft (forward: deeper jib, closed leech; aft: twist off the top) and athwartships (the sheeting angle). You can only move the lazy (windward) car; it takes effect after the tack.
  - The halyards are set before the race and left alone.
- **How the crew sets them** follows North Sails' Nordic Folkboat tuning guide: the outhaul 3 cm off the band below 5 kt, 1.5 cm to 12 kt, on the band above; no Cunningham in light air, just enough to clear the wrinkles in medium, hard above 15 kt; the traveller cars 15–20 cm to windward below 6 kt, in the middle to 14 kt, eased to leeward above; the vang never upwind below 18 kt. On top of that, in a breeze, the crew works toward the depowering step that's fastest for their boat now: twice a second the sim tries each step (backstay, Cunningham and downhaul, vang, jib cars aft, the traveller and boom eased), finds where she'd heel with the crew hiking, and weighs the sails' drive less the keel's drag for the side force (ORC's induced drag, with ORC's extra for a boat sailed nearly upright) and the rudder's drag against her weather helm, as ORC's VPP picks its crew position and flattening. The same search for the polar's standard crew of three is the reference: a smaller or lighter crew that has to flatten further is slower in a breeze, a heavier one a little faster. A skilled crew follows the fastest step closely, a novice only once it's well off. (By the physics alone she'd carry full power to about 17 kt; the guide flattens from 12–15 kt. Why the two differ is on the ROADMAP.)
- **Shape.** The lines set each sail's depth, draft position and twist; the wind blows the draft aft as it builds. The fastest shape depends on the wind and the point of sail, following standard trim practice: full with some twist in light air; full power with the leech closed in medium; flat, draft forward and twisted to spill power in a breeze; fuller off the wind with the vang controlling twist. Missing it costs up to about 8% of drive. The 3D sails show the depth, draft and twist.
- **Who trims: one line at a time.** The crew trims every line until you work one. Work any line (its Trim/Ease buttons, a key, its −/+ in **Lines**, or drag its bar) and that line, and only that line, is yours; the crew keeps working everything else for the conditions. Lines you have show in amber with an **Auto** button: one tap gives that line back to the crew, who carry on from where it is. The **Trim** button shows how many lines you have ("Trim · you ×2 · all to crew") and gives them all back in one click. **Lines** lists every line with its setting, grouped as on the boat, so you can watch what the crew does too. **[ ]** select a line, **; '** less/more (hold); the sheets also on Z/X and , / . The buttons and bars are big enough to hit without looking twice.
- **Drag the bars** in the Lines list to where you want a line: it's hauled or eased there at the usual pace (the jib sheets on their winches, the others by hand), with a white marker showing where it's going.
- **Both jib sheets.** Each jib sheet has its own winch and its own row: the leeward sheet trims the jib, and the windward (lazy) one, normally slack, can be hauled in to back the jib, its clew held to windward at the windward car (**Back** in the trim panel hauls it home; **Release** lets it go). The windward sheet runs across in front of the mast, so it only bites once it's hauled well in. The sheets belong to their sides: at a tack the crew lets the old sheet fly and tails the new one, unless you're holding the windward sheet, when the sheets swap roles as they really do, and a jib sheet you'd trimmed and didn't release ends up backing the jib on the new tack.
- **The shape readout** (with the Lines list open) says what's off and which line fixes it: "Main: too full (outhaul, backstay on), draft aft (Cunningham, downhaul on) · Jib: shape good · shape 97%".
- **On the boat:** the traveller on its thwart across the cockpit, the backstay bridle and its control led to cleats at the thwart ends (pulled down as it goes on), the vang, the winches, the jib cars on their tracks and the sheets led through them (the lazy one slack), and the piano's cleats and lines.
- **Telltales** on your boat: three pairs on the jib luff (red on the port side of the sail, green on starboard, as on real sails), and one on the main leech at each batten. Windward ones lift and dance when you're luffing; leeward ones droop when stalled; leech telltales curl behind the main when it's over-trimmed; downwind they hang. Your sails are slightly see-through so the leeward telltales show through, as on real Dacron.
- **Sail shape detail.** A luff bubble when under-trimmed, flogging when let go, three battens, and a long, low rectangular window in the main parallel to the boom (1.8 m × 0.35 m, 0.3 m above it).

### Instruments

- **The instrument row** is styled on real displays: the race clock, boatspeed (and speed over the ground), the **windex** as you'd see it looking up at the masthead (the vane into the apparent wind, the close-hauled tabs, the boat bow-up), true wind speed on the water (its direction under it, with lifts, headers, puffs and lulls), and the tide where you are (flood or ebb, in knots, with the chop and swell under it). The windex box names the point of sail (close-hauled, close reach, beam reach, broad reach, run).
- The **wind-angle dial** (true and apparent wind around a boat outline) is a training aid real boats don't have, so it's hidden by default; turn it on in Setup under Training aids.

- **Tactical compass** under the instrument pods: a tape of headings around yours, with the bearing to the next mark (yellow), the wind direction (blue), the heading you'd sail on the other tack (or gybe) after you turn, and your average close-hauled heading on this tack ("avg"). Sailing higher than your average on starboard (lower on port) means you're lifted; the tape says "lifted 6°" or "headed 4°". That's how racers spot shifts on the compass: tack on the headers.
- **Transits** (with training aids on): how racers sight the line and the laylines. Before the start, the far end of the line lines up with something ashore (a landmark if one is in line, otherwise the point where the line runs onto the far shore, e.g. "the shore of Angel Island" for the Seaweed Soup line); when they're in line, you're on the line. On a beat, the mark lines up with something behind it along your layline. The hint under the line box says what to line up and how far off you are ("X is 3° left of it" or "in line now"), and a faint dashed line on the water shows the line's transit.
- **The bulkhead compass** on the boat has a card that turns with the heading (you can read it from the cockpit view).
- **Windex** on the masthead: it swings into the apparent wind, with two reference tabs set at the close-hauled angle either side of the bow.

### Heaving to

- **How.** Close-hauled, tap **Back** (or haul the windward jib sheet): the crew lets the leeward sheet fly and the jib backs to windward. Ease the main until it just draws (Ease, or drag the mainsheet). Once she's slowed to a knot or two, hold the tiller to leeward and **Lash tiller** (**I**): it stays there, hands off. **◀ ▶** beside it move the lashing; taking the tiller (T, a click, A/D) unlashes it. Lash it to leeward while she still has way on and she'll round up and tack, as a real boat would. Or the classic way: hold both jib sheets and tack without letting go. A slack lazy sheet comes in hand over hand in a second or two; only the last of it, under load, needs the winch.
- **The physics** (`physics.ts`, `heaveTo`). With the jib aback the polar no longer applies, so her motion comes from the forces, forward, sideways and in yaw: the backed jib as a stalled plate (its force square to its chord, Cn ≈ 1.2 sin α, Hoerner), pushing the bow off and shoving her to leeward; the main from its trim and shape as when sailing; the hull and long keel as a low-aspect wing with way on and, as she slows, broadside drag strip by strip along the keel (which also damps her turning); the rudder at its own angle of attack, less the water coming at it from leeward as she slips; windage of hull and rig; heel and the crew's weight as when sailing. As she bears away the main fills and the rudder bites and turn her up; as she comes up the main luffs and the backed jib pushes her off. She lies about 50–60° off the wind, creeping ahead at around a knot (0.7 kn in 8 kt, about 1.5 kn in 20) and slipping to leeward at about as much, so she makes good a track roughly square to the wind. The main sets how high she lies; a light lashing lets her fore-reach faster. Her drive is the sails' raw force at a crawl, easing toward the polar's calibration as she nears the polar speed (the force model leaves out the heeled, yawed and wave resistance that grows there), so a backed jib can never make her faster than the polar: it always slows her. The crew sets the lines it has around the ones you hold.
- **Checked against** the figures we could find, none of them measurements: Folkboat and IF-boat owners report about 1–1.5 kn fore-reaching hove to; seamanship guides put the bow 40–60° off the wind and the drift at 1–2 kn in a blow. tests/heaveto.test.ts holds her inside those bands from 8 to 25 kt on both tacks. Better figures (a GPS track of a real Folkboat hove to) would tighten them.

### The crew: weight, skill and work

- **Everyone has a place** (`src/boat/crewPhys.ts`): across the boat and fore and aft, moving at a human pace after a human reaction time (an expert ~0.4 s, a novice ~1.6 s), and the boat answers every move: heel and righting moment, fore-and-aft trim, helm, speed. Your crew's height, weight and skill (novice, club racer, expert) are set in Setup; each club boat's AI crew has a skill of its own.
- **What a good crew does:** to leeward in light air, heeling her a few degrees (in a drifter the sails' own weight is about as much as the wind's pressure on them, and a little heel lets gravity hold them in shape); sitting up to windward in a breeze; hiking only when she'd heel past her best; flat downwind, the bow hand forward at the mast in light air; through a tack, a **roll tack**: they hold the old high side as she comes through the wind, so she heels onto the new leeward side, then cross fast and hike to snap her flat. The rig sweeping to windward through the air gives the sails a pull (the roll's speed times their height, added to the apparent wind); in light air the crew, sitting to leeward, move up onto the old high side as she comes through the wind so she still rolls. The rig sweeping through the air adds push while she rolls, and heeled to leeward her weather helm starts the turn with less rudder; on a 2.3-tonne keelboat these come to very little (on a Laser, measured with GPS, a roll tack is 3–5% faster through the tack), the flatten stops once she's upright, and it never leaves her faster than before the tack (RRS 42.3(b)). A novice sits in the middle in light air, lets her heel more before hiking, and crosses late and slowly.
- **Hiking, by the class rules:** upwind, the crew's legs from mid-thigh down stay inboard of the sheer, and no straps or hiking aids are allowed (NFIA Class Rules 2025–2028, CR 13.20), so a hiker sits on the deck edge, legs in, and leans the body out: about 1.35 m out for a 1.75 m sailor, against 0.8 m sitting up (from body-segment proportions). Hiking tires the thighs: a few minutes flat out and they lean out less, recovering off the rail.
- **Fore and aft** (`src/boat/hullTrim.ts`): the crew's weight trims her (two crew a metre forward: about 5 cm by the bow). A little bow-down lifts the transom clear (less wetted surface: pays in light air); more ploughs the bow into her own wave once she's moving fast, so the best trim moves aft as she speeds up. Bow-down also moves the keel's grip forward: a touch more weather helm. Worth up to about 1% of speed. (The magnitudes are estimates: no Folkboat hydrostatics were to be had; the directions are sailing practice.)
- **Who does what, and when** (`src/boat/crewWork.ts`): three aboard, the helm steers and has the mainsheet and backstay, the middle crew the jib and traveller, the bow hand the cabin-top lines (vang, outhaul, Cunninghams, downhaul), the jib cars and the pole; with two, the crew does it all, one job after another; alone, the helm does everything with one hand, and has to leave the tiller's end to reach the cabin top. Each person works one thing at a time: they react, get there (their weight goes with them: in off the rail to the lines, to the leeward winch to tail the sheet after a tack in light air, out of the cockpit to the car), then work it. Sheets come first. A skilled crew reacts sooner, works faster and makes finer adjustments; nobody gets up for a change that isn't worth it, or starts on a control line in the middle of a tack.
- **The jib cars are rarely moved.** Moving one means crawling forward to the cabin-top track, pulling the pin, sliding the car and pinning it again; the working (leeward) car can't be moved while the sheet loads it. So the crew moves only the lazy car, before the next tack, and only for a change that has lasted (half a minute to a minute and a half, by skill), not while she needs that person's weight on the rail. When you move the car (Lines), the crew goes and does it.
- **You can place the crew yourself** (the trim panel's Crew row, or 7 8 9 0): they stay there, still going where their jobs take them, until you give it back to them.

### Heel, helm balance and the trim coach

- **Heel comes from the forces on the sails** (`src/boat/balance.ts`): each sail's force from the apparent wind, its area, its angle of attack (sheet and traveller), its depth (outhaul, backstay, Cunningham, and the vang bending the lower mast a little) and its twist (sheet tension and vang). The part across the boat, high up the rig, heels her until the hull's righting moment and the crew's weight, where they are, balance it (she can heel to windward too: crew up on the rail and little wind in the sails). The righting moment is from three Folkboats' ORC certificates (their measured righting moment at 1° and centre of gravity: GM about 0.52 m in sailing trim with the crew aboard). She rolls to it as a damped oscillator (inertia and damping from Klaka's full-scale tests of a yacht at rest, plus the keel's lift damping her roll when she's moving), so a gust or a crew move rolls her over in a second or so. A gust heels her at once; depowering stands her up. Upwind with the crew's settings: about 6° in 4 kt (heeled to leeward on purpose), 8° at 10 kt, 14° at 12 kt, 16° at 14 kt with the crew hiking, about 19° at 18 kt and 21° from 20 kt as the crew depowers; past about 20° she slows (the heeled hull).
- **Weather helm.** The main's force acts aft of the keel and turns her toward the wind, the jib's forward and turns her away; heel moves the drive to leeward, and an eased main pushes from well outboard, both turning her toward the wind. The rudder angle that cancels it is her helm. **Let go of the tiller and she rounds up** (weather helm) or bears away (lee helm), as a sailor tests it. Two or three degrees of weather helm is ideal; more is a brake. The trim panel shows heel and helm (e.g. "Heel 18° · Helm 4° weather") with what to do: weather helm comes off with the controls that don't cost pointing first, backstay on and traveller down, then easing the main. The AI holds whatever helm its boat needs (and pays the drag).
- **Trim coach**: a teacher, one thing at a time. Its headline sits under the sails' state in the trim panel and, when it has something new to say, shows on the water for a few seconds: the single correction worth the most speed right now ("Trim the jib in, about 13°."), or "Trim's right: 5.1 kt against the polar's 5.1." when it is. The **Trim coach** button opens the rest: the situation (point of sail, wind band, sea, overpowered, or the phase: tacking, pre-start, rounding, jib aback), one short reason, **Look at**: the tell on the boat that shows it (the telltales, the battens, the heel, the weight on the tiller), and one line on what the crew is doing with their lines. Smaller things wait behind a toggle. When you fix it, it says so with the speed it made ("That's it: jib telltales streaming both sides, 3.5 → 3.9 kt.") and moves on. It coaches the lines you hold; the crew's are theirs (in a breeze the crew eases the main deliberately to keep her on her feet, and it says so). Each lesson's weight comes from the boat's own equations: every line you hold is tried across its range (angle of attack, the sail's shape against the best shape for the conditions, heel and helm), so a line that costs nothing (the traveller up in 20 kt, with the crew easing the sheet to hold the boom) isn't nagged about. It also coaches steering (pinching, feathering when everything's on) and heaving to, step by step.
- **Sourced, and still estimated.** Sourced: the righting moment at small angles and the centre of gravity (ORC certificates), the rig's dimensions and the centre of effort (certificates and the class rig drawing), the sails' forces (ORC's model), the wind's gradient and the heeled wind triangle (ORC), the keel's induced drag (ORC's formula), the crew's line settings (North Sails' guide), hiking (the class rules and body-segment data). The wetted surface as she heels comes from the Delft Systematic Yacht Hull Series regression: for a hull as narrow and deep as the Folkboat's it changes by well under 1% at 5–10°, so heeling her to leeward in light air pays through the sails' shape, not the wetted area. Estimated, for want of published data: the righting curve past a few degrees (the textbook wall-sided formula, with the centre of buoyancy's height estimated) and the hull's extra drag when heeled (ORC works both from the hull's offsets, which ORC and the NFIA hold but don't publish), the keel's effective draft (ORC's formula, with the full draft standing in for the reduced draft ORC computes), the hull's fore-and-aft trim hydrodynamics, how fast sails age, how a mainsheet's slant opens the leech, and the crew's reaction times and work rates.

Later in the coming week, whether that forecast has fog, in its own words ("patchy fog", "chance of fog"), with no figure. Where there's nothing real, the layer says so ("no data", or "no fog forecast") instead of drawing fog. The 3D fog bank is still an estimate, and fog doesn't change the sailing (ROADMAP).
- **Contrast:** your boat is cyan, bigger, with a ring; the other boats are in their own colours; the course, the start line, the marks and your destination are vivid orange: neither colour is used by the chart. Everything the sim draws sits on a halo (white on the paper colours, black on dusk) with a dark edge, so it reads over the chart's soundings and lines.
- **Where the chart and the sim disagree** (`tests/chart.test.ts` measures it): over the race area 0.8% of the water and land differ (1.1% before the coast from Fort Point to Crissy Field came from OpenStreetMap, 1.9% before the Wharf and Pier 39), spread round the Marin shore and Angel Island in the terrain's 50 m cells, and off the Wharf. The navigation buoys the sim uses (Harding Rock, Blossom Rock, North Channel 1, 2 and 4) are within 30 m of NOAA's positions; the private racing buoys differ (see Known gaps).

### Seeing the wind, the course and the laylines

- **Cassette-tape telltales.** Both upper shrouds carry a length of old cassette tape about 1.4 m above the deck, where you can see it from the cockpit. Each streams the way the apparent wind blows, droops when it's light (under ~6 kt apparent) and flutters.
- **Free look.** In cockpit and chase view, the arrow keys (or Q/E and R/F) turn your head. Looking never steers. W or the "Look ahead" chip faces forward again.
- **Course view.** "View the course" on the setup screen, or **Course** / **M** during the race (the race keeps running). It opens on one leg (your current one) with the others faded; step through with ◀ ▶, tap a leg in the list, or show the whole course. Drag to move, scroll or pinch to zoom. It shows every leg in order with numbers, the way round each rounding mark (green clockwise arc = leave to starboard, red counter-clockwise = leave to port), the side to pass each pass mark, the start/finish line, zones, laylines, wind, current and boats, plus a plain-language list of the legs with your current one highlighted.
- **Laylines.** Dashed lines on the water and on both maps for your next rounding mark: green = starboard-tack layline, red = port-tack. On beats they use the polar's beat angle; on runs, the best downwind angle (wing-on-wing). Both allow for the current at the mark. Before your start they point at the first mark. Turned off with the training aids.

### Changes from the prototype

The port keeps the prototype's behaviour. These differences are deliberate:

- Three.js r128 → r187. Colour management is turned off and light intensities are scaled by π so the scene looks the same as before.
- Sub-steps are now exactly 1/60 s (they used to be "up to 1/60 s"), and replay snapshots are exactly every 0.1 s.
- AI decisions and boat placement use a seeded RNG instead of `Math.random`.
- Added `[hidden]{display:none}` and `box-sizing` rules. The prototype relied on its host page for these; standalone, the hidden overlays covered the screen.

## Known gaps

- Acceleration, slowing and turning are calibrated from physics and sailing judgement, not measured on a real Folkboat. If your boat feels different, the constants in `HANDLING` are the place to adjust.
- Above 24 kt the polar is held flat (no depowering model yet). No sail trim beyond automatic.
- AI starts: on the Seaweed Soup line, median 5–11 s after the gun in slack and ebb, ~25 s in flood, almost never over early. On the short training line they're slower (crowding). The AI keeps clear, takes penalties in clear water, won't tack when too slow to make it, and doesn't sail on a broad reach expecting to slow down by easing sails.
- Within each 6-minute observation (or each forecast hour), wind shifts are a synthetic oscillation; on a past day the wind across the course comes from shore stations (none on the Marin side, Angel Island or Alcatraz), so the north side of the course is mostly the terrain model's; the open-water factor (the station reads less than the open water) is fitted for the westerly and used for every wind; the lee of the city in a southerly isn't modelled along the Cityfront (the station is there); puffs are random (seeded), not forecast. Current between the 34 NOAA stations is interpolated, not modelled hydrodynamically, and it's the predicted tide: on the day, river outflow and wind change it a little.
- The racing marks against NOAA's chart (`src/sim/places.ts`): the St. Francis outer buoy "A" is 136 m west of the private buoy NOAA charts as "A", and "X" 66 m from NOAA's "X"; Blackaller (16) is 148 m from NOAA's private buoy "C"; Knox (4), Fort Mason "M" (6) and Hank Easom (15) have no charted buoy near them (the nearest are Raccoon Strait 4, 1.0 km; East Yacht Harbor Light 2, 0.5 km; Yellow Bluff Light, 0.5 km). Racing marks are YRA and club marks, so a mismatch may be the chart or the marks list: not moved.
- The shoreline the boats sail against is OpenStreetMap's from the Gate's south shore round Fort Point to Pier 27 (Crissy Field, the Marina, its harbors, Fort Mason's piers, Aquatic Park, Fisherman's Wharf, Pier 39 and Piers 35 to 27), and hand-traced east of Pier 27; the terrain is real, and is fitted to it along the Cityfront. The open piers, breakwaters and floating docks at the Wharf (Municipal Pier, Hyde Street Pier, Pier 47, Pier 41, the Wharf's breakwater, Pier 39's breakwaters and marina docks) are solid and drawn, but the wind and current treat the shore there as the coastline only: the current flows under the open piers, and their wind shadow isn't modelled. Marin, Angel Island, Alcatraz and Treasure Island are solid, from the 50 m terrain grid.
- Rules not modelled yet: 14 (beyond the swerve check), 18.1(a)(2) (opposite tacks when only one boat must tack at the mark), 19 with another boat as the obstruction, 20.3 (passing on a hail), 22 (capsized, anchored or aground), and 43.1(b) and (c) beyond what the precedence already gives. Boats blanket each other's wind, but Rule 23's interference check only covers physical interference. Boats can't back their sails, so Rule 21.3 never comes up.
- The GGYC start line's inner end (the race-deck marker) is on the jetty's seawall in front of the clubhouse, from OpenStreetMap's footprint; its exact spot on the deck, and the line's wording in GGYC's current sailing instructions, weren't reachable (only summaries of past SIs: "an orange marker on the race deck" to X). The line is about 410 m on 351° to the YRA's X.
- Rule 20.1(a)'s "substantial course change" has no figure in the rules or World Sailing's cases: the sim takes 10°, after Dave Perry's guideline. Rule 20.3 (passing a hail on) isn't modelled, so the island-mark exception looks only at the boat you'd hail.
- Fouls between two AI boats are penalized but not explained.
